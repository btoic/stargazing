# Tutorial: Astronomical Darkness, Twilight & Ephemeris Planning

Timing an astronomical observation session requires understanding the relationship between the Sun's position below the horizon, the Moon's phase and rise/set times, and atmospheric extinction.

---

## 1. The Stages of Twilight

Twilight occurs while sunlight continues to scatter through the upper atmosphere after sunset or before sunrise. Astronomers define three distinct stages based on solar depression angle:

| Twilight Stage | Solar Altitude ($\text{Alt}_\odot$) | Sky Conditions & Observing Feasibility |
| :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Sunset** | $0.0^\circ$ | Sun disappears below true horizon. |
| **Civil Twilight** | $0.0^\circ \rightarrow -6.0^\circ$ | Horizon and terrestrial objects clearly visible. Brightest planets (Venus, Jupiter) and 1st mag stars appear. Mirror cooldown period. |
| **Nautical Twilight** | $-6.0^\circ \rightarrow -12.0^\circ$ | Horizon becomes difficult to distinguish at sea. General constellation outlines appear. Ideal for high-contrast binary stars (Albireo). |
| **Astronomical Twilight** | $-12.0^\circ \rightarrow -18.0^\circ$ | Sky darkens substantially. Milky Way dust clouds become noticeable. Fainter stars (to mag 5) emerge. Globular clusters become easily visible. |
| **True Astronomical Darkness** | $\le -18.0^\circ$ | **Maximum natural darkness.** Zero solar illumination scattered in the upper atmosphere. The mandatory window for faint deep-sky nebulae, external galaxies, and dark rifts. |

## 2. Moon Ephemeris & Lunar Interference

The Moon is the single most powerful source of natural light pollution. Even a quarter moon will wash out faint emission nebulae and galaxy spiral arms.

### Evaluating Lunar Impact:
1. **Waxing / Waning Crescent (1% – 5% illumination)**:
   - Sets shortly after sunset (or rises shortly before dawn).
   - Once set, provides 100% moonless, dark sky conditions.
2. **First Quarter / Gibous / Full Moon**:
   - Casts bright shadows and brightens the sky background to Bortle 7–8 equivalent.
   - Restricts observing to double stars, open clusters, the Moon itself, and bright planets.
3. **Session Rule**: Always verify moonset time relative to the observation start time. True deep-sky observing requires the Moon to be **below the horizon ($\text{Alt}_\text{Moon} < 0^\circ$)**.

---

## 3. Atmospheric Extinction & Horizon Obstruction Thresholds

When viewing celestial objects close to the horizon, their light travels through a much thicker column of Earth's atmosphere ("airmass"):
- **At Zenith (90° Alt)**: Airmass = $1.0$. Minimal extinction (~0.15–0.25 mag loss).
- **At 30° Altitude**: Airmass = $2.0$. Extinction ~0.3–0.5 mag.
- **At 15° Altitude**: Airmass = $3.8$. Extinction ~0.8–1.2 mag; severe thermal boiling and color fringing.
- **Below 10° Altitude**: Airmass > $5.5$. Objects dim dramatically and shimmer wildly due to ground-level convection.

### The Stargazing Golden Rules:
1. **The 15° Hard Floor**: Never schedule faint deep-sky targets below 15° altitude.
2. **The 20° Sweet Spot**: Aim to observe targets when they transit within **$\pm 1.5$ hours of the meridian** at their maximum nightly altitude.
3. **Terrain Obstruction**: Always add local tree and terrain elevation (e.g. Dvigrad's 10°–15° valley ridge) to the astronomical horizon cutoff.
